\section{Regression Analysis}
\label{sec:regression}

%%  MAX 1.5 PAGES

The pace of the walmuur programme determines how long the 17 walls of
Section~\ref{sec:prob} keep their timber piles, and therefore how much risk
the municipality carries towards 2050. The programme plan assumes about
100\,m of wall per year \parencite{utrecht_herstelwerven}. The team tested
this against the renewal year of each of the 32 renewed walls
(Section~\ref{sec:data}), which gives the renewed length per year in
Table~\ref{tab:production}, 3{,}431\,m in total against the 3{,}356\,m that
the municipality reports \parencite{gu2025up}.

The first hypothesis is that the pace is constant. A linear regression
\begin{equation}
L_y = a + b\,(y - 2015) + \varepsilon_y \label{eq:production}
\end{equation}
where $L_y$ [m] is the length renewed in year $y$, $a$ [m] the intercept,
$b$ [m/yr per year] the trend and $\varepsilon_y$ [m] an independent normal
residual, gives $b = -55$\,m/yr per year (95\,\% confidence interval
\textminus105 to \textminus4, $p = 0.04$, $R^2 = 0.37$). The hypothesis of a
constant pace is therefore rejected at the 5\,\% level: the programme has
slowed down. The residuals, however, show a break rather than a gradual
decline. From 2015 to 2018 the programme renewed 607\,m per year, whereas since 2019 the pace has been 125\,m per year (95\,\%
confidence interval 22 to 228\,m), with three years without any completed
wall. Extrapolating the straight line would predict negative production
after 2027, so the line is used only as a test. The second hypothesis, that
the recent pace equals the 100\,m per year of the plan, is not rejected
($p = 0.58$), but the interval is too wide to confirm it.

To predict the backlog, the team resampled the yearly production of 2019 to
2026 (bootstrap, 20{,}000 runs) and renewed the 17 modelled walls one after
the other from 2027. The risk that a wall carries until its renewal year $T_i$
follows from its best-estimate failure probability $P_{f,i}(t)$ over the
15-year reference period (Section~\ref{sec:prob}), taken linear between 2026
and 2041:
\begin{equation}
E[N] = \sum_{i} \left[1 - \exp\!\left(-\sum_{t=2026}^{T_i} h_i(t)\right)\right],
\qquad h_i(t) = -\frac{\ln\bigl(1 - P_{f,i}(t)\bigr)}{15}, \label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
where $E[N]$ [--] is the expected number of walls that reach a limit state
before they are renewed and $h_i(t)$ [1/yr] the yearly hazard of wall $i$,
which assumes that failures occur at a constant rate within a reference
period. Table~\ref{tab:backlog} compares three production paces and two
orders of work: the walls with the lowest $\beta$ first, or a random order
that ignores risk.

Table~\ref{tab:backlog} shows that at the recent pace the modelled walls are
renewed around 2031, well before 2050, and that about one of the 17 walls is
expected to reach a limit state before its renewal. The pace matters more
than the order: renewing the walls with the lowest $\beta$ first lowers $E[N]$
by about 10\,\%, whereas a return to the pace of 2016 to 2018 would lower it by
about 45\,\%. For the 780\,m of walmuur that the municipality still has to
restore \parencite{gu2025up}, the recent mean pace gives completion around
2033, but the lower bound of its interval, 22\,m per year, would push
completion beyond 2050. These predictions rest on few data: twelve years, of
which three without a completed wall, and a completion year that ignores
work in progress, so the bootstrap treats years as independent although
projects span several years. Moreover, $E[N]$ inherits the conservative bias of
the failure model (Section~\ref{sec:prob-validity}), and a limit state calls
for an emergency measure rather than meaning collapse. The comparison between
the scenarios is therefore more reliable than the absolute numbers;
Section~\ref{sec:results-recommendations} uses it to rank the options of the
municipality.

\section{Results Interpretation and Proposed Recommendations}
\label{sec:results-recommendations}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
%%  MAX 1.5 PAGES
%%  Task: discuss the results against the abstraction of the models, the
%%  data uncertainty and the assumptions; justify their soundness; give
%%  recommendations for non-experts (interventions, benefits, costs,
%%  uncertainties, risks, synergies); state what is known and assumed,
%%  the programming direction, what to measure next, and which
%%  recommendations need an institutional change rather than money.

The analysis gives the municipality three results. First, the asset register
overstates the backlog of walmuren: 11 of the 29 walls that it shows as past
their design life have already been renewed on steel piles, and only 17
walls, 611\,m in total, still stand on their original timber piles with a
werf (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Second, all 17 have a best-estimate
reliability index below 1.8, the lowest rejection level of NEN~8700, both now
and in 2041 (Section~\ref{sec:prob}), so under the current rules each would be
red in any consequence class. Third, at the pace of the last eight years
these walls will all be renewed around 2031, and until then about one of
them is expected to reach a limit state (Section~\ref{sec:regression}); per
wall, the programme fails to prevent this with a probability of 0.13 over 15
years (Section~\ref{sec:fault-tree}).

These numbers are less certain than their precision suggests, and the
uncertainty is not evenly spread. Known are which walls are renewed, the werf
level of 14 walls and the fact that every wall stands. Assumed are the wall
type, the condition of the piles, the soil class and the load on the werf,
together with two schematic cross-sections drawn for the Oudegracht. The wall
type alone changes $\beta$ by about 2, more than any other input
(Section~\ref{sec:dimension-reduction}), and the type-1 model lets standing
walls fail in up to two thirds of the draws, which suggests that it is
conservative. The model also leaves out settlement from pile rot, the damage
actually observed, and a limit state in the model calls for an emergency
measure rather than meaning collapse. The model's 0.17 failing walls per year
has the same order of magnitude as the roughly 0.5 incidents per year
observed in the whole Wervengebied (Section~\ref{sec:fault-tree}). The
results are therefore sound as a ranking of walls and measures and as an
order of magnitude, but not as absolute safety levels.

Table~\ref{tab:recommendations} turns this into recommendations. The first two
are measurements, because the dominant uncertainties are cheap to reduce
compared with a renewal of about \euro1.3 million per wall, and they decide
which walls are red. Finding type~2 on the three sand walls of rak~4 to 6
(197\,m) would raise their $\beta$ from about 0.8 to 2.0, above the lowest
level, whereas finding type~1 on Oude Gracht Rak~03 OZ would confirm it as the
most urgent wall ($\beta$ \textminus0.70). The third concerns the pace, which
matters more than the order of work: the expected number of walls that reach a
limit state before renewal is about 1.0 at the recent pace and 0.6 at the pace
of 2016 to 2018, whereas ordering by $\beta$ saves 10\,\% at no cost. The fourth, the cheapest technical measure of Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff},
does not pay for itself in expected value at a failure cost of \euro1.5
million, but it lowers the failure probability of a wall by about a quarter for
less than 10\,\% of the cost of renewal, which a risk-averse owner of heritage
walls may prefer.

The last two recommendations need an institutional change rather than money.
A register that keeps the year of the original wall makes the design-life rule
flag walls renewed ten years ago, so renewals, foundation and wall type should
be recorded where the screening reads them. The municipality also has to
decide which consequence class applies to a walmuur and whether a
probabilistic assessment with proven strength is accepted, because these
choices, not new data, decide whether a wall with a $\beta$ of 1.5 is red.
Finally, combining the dives with the inspections of the wharf cellars, and
the renewals with planned street and sewer works, would disrupt each rak only
once.
