\textbf{Top event.} The function of the quay wall programme is to keep the walls from failing. The top event is therefore that the programme does not prevent the failure of a wall. It is a rich event, with technical, human, organisational and external causes. We analyse one wall, the median of the 17 modelled walls, over the 15 years of the NEN 8700 reference period~\cite{nen8700}. ``Failure'' means that a limit state of the model in Section~\ref{sec:prob-ls} is reached. In practice this calls for an emergency measure or a closure; it is not necessarily a collapse.
 
\textbf{Structure.} Figure~\ref{fig:faulttree} shows the tree. The top event is an OR gate with two branches. \emph{Load too high} groups soil, water, surcharge and sudden events. \emph{Resistance too low} has two mechanisms that match the two limit states of Section~\ref{sec:prob-ls}: pile bending and overturning. Both are AND gates: a weak pile or wall only causes failure if the weakening is not repaired in time. That happens when the weakness is not recognised (screening AND inspection miss it) or when the repair comes too late. This event feeds both mechanisms and is drawn once; WT (wall type) and GW (groundwater) appear twice as the same event. The programme cannot prevent load events.

\textbf{Probabilities.} Each basic event has a probability for one wall over 15 years, a range and a label for its basis (Table~\ref{tab:ftprob}). Every value that does not come from data or literature is labelled \emph{Assumption}: it is our own judgement.

Eight events are already inside the failure model of Section~\ref{sec:prob}~\cite{hemel2023,hemel2024}: G1, G2, P1, P3, WT, GW, M1 and M2. Giving them a second probability would count them twice, so they are combined into one event M, ``wall reaches a limit state in the model'', with $p=\Phi(-\beta)$ from Table~\ref{tab:sens-walls}. For the median wall, Oude Gracht Rak~09 WZ, this is 0.15; the range 0.13--0.15 comes from the sensitivity columns of that table. The model gives this probability without any repair. Section~\ref{sec:prob-validity} states that the model shows a ranking and order of magnitude, not exact values. Using M as a probability in the tree is therefore an assumption. The other events get one of four bases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Data:} S2 is 0 for this wall, because 16 of the 17 modelled walls are past their design life and are flagged by the register (Table~\ref{tab:selection}).
  \item \emph{Derived:} E3 follows from one reported scour case~\cite{wijkbericht2024walmuur} in the 51 walls of the register over 6 years: $1/(51 \times 6) \times 15 = 0.05$. The window of 6 years is assumed. L1 follows from the production rate of Section~\ref{sec:regression}: at the pace of 2019 to 2026, the 611\,m of the 17 walls are renewed before 2041 in more than 99.9\,\% of the bootstrap runs, so practically no wall is out of reach within the reference period (0.00); only at the lower bound of that pace, 22\,m per year, would 45\,\% not be reached. This assumes that a wall not reached in 15 years is repaired too late; the risk a wall carries while it waits is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:regression}.
  \item \emph{Literature:} I2 and I3 are generic HEART values~\cite{williams1986heart,heart_table_plos2021}. The task type (C: complex task needing skill; M: miscellaneous) is our choice, and the values are not specific to Utrecht.
  \item \emph{Assumption:} for the other events we found no data. We use a fixed scale: extremely rare 0.001, very rare 0.005, rare 0.02, occasional 0.10, likely 0.30, each with the range in Table~\ref{tab:ftprob}. The sources in the table only show that an event type occurs, not how often. A load event counts only if it occurs \emph{and} brings the wall to its limit state: one heavy vehicle does not fail a wall. Apart from the scour case, the sources contain no such failure, so Q1 and E2 are very rare and the other load events extremely rare. For I1 the programme ends in 2030~\cite{utrecht_teamchallenge2026}, so 11 of the 15 years have no planned inspection; 0.73 is therefore the upper bound and 0.30 our choice.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Propagation.} For one wall we use $P = 1-\prod_i(1-p_i)$ for an OR gate and $P=\prod_i p_i$ for an AND gate~\cite{vesely1981ftHandbook}. Within one wall, WT and GW sit inside the model event, where they are drawn once, so their dependence is kept. ``Weakening not repaired in time'' is one event that feeds both mechanisms, so it is not counted twice. For the 17 walls together we use Monte Carlo simulation ($4\times10^5$ runs), because some causes act on several walls at once. The model events of the walls are linked with a Gaussian copula with $\rho=0.084$, a weak dependence that an earlier version of the failure model gave for two neighbouring sections; the current model simulates the walls independently (Section~\ref{sec:prob-dependence}), so this value is an assumption. S3, I1 and I2 are partly shared by all walls (same screening model, same programme, same inspectors): a share of 100\,\%, 100\,\% and 50\,\% of their probability is one common draw for all walls, the rest is drawn per wall. This keeps the probability per wall the same. These shares, the value of $\rho$, and the independence of the load events between walls are our assumptions.

\textbf{Results.} The load branch gives 0.06 and ``weakening not repaired in time'' 0.35. The probability for one wall is 0.11 (0.04 with all probabilities at their lower bound, 0.30 at their upper bound; 0.09 to 0.23 over the 17 walls). If no weakening were ever repaired in time, it would be 0.20, so the repair chain lowers it by 0.09. Over the 17 walls the expected number of failing walls in 15 years is 2.1. Common cause does not change this mean and widens the spread only slightly: the standard deviation goes from 1.41 to 1.46 and the probability of at least 5 failing walls from 0.058 to 0.064. There are 19 minimal cut sets: eight of order 1 (the load events), five of order 2 and six of order 3 (the model event with one unrepaired path). The Fussell--Vesely importance~\cite{fussell1975} is 0.43 for the model event, 0.42 for scour E3 and 0.09 each for L2 to L4; all others are 0.04 or less, and L1 contributes nothing at its base value. Moving one probability from its lower to its upper bound changes the result most for E3 (0.12) and L1 (0.04), the others by 0.02 or less.

\textbf{Do the results match our beliefs and the data?} Roughly. The sources name about four incidents in the whole Wervengebied between 2017 and 2024: a temporary support at Oudegracht 110--122 (2017)~\cite{duic2022}, a partly closed street on the Kromme Nieuwegracht and a road hole on the Nieuwegracht (reported in 2020)~\cite{duic2020}, and scour under the wall near the Bezembrug (2024)~\cite{wijkbericht2024walmuur}. That is about 0.5 per year, all emergency measures and no collapse. The tree gives 0.14 per year for the 17 modelled walls, which are only part of the area: the same order of magnitude. A first version, in which a load event counted as failure as soon as it occurred, gave 6.3 failures for the 17 walls alone and was rejected. Our incident search was not systematic, so four is a lower bound, and the model event is on the safe side: the type-1 model is conservative (Section~\ref{sec:prob-validity}) and 7\,kPa is a heavy load for a werf (Section~\ref{sec:data-effect}). For collapse, the probabilities would be much lower.

\textbf{Why most probabilities are assumptions.} Of the 19 events in Table~\ref{tab:ftprob}, one comes from the model, one from data, two are derived from data and two come from generic literature; the other 13 are assumptions. There are four reasons for this. First, organisational events such as a delayed permit, late consent of an owner or a skipped inspection are recorded only in internal files of the municipality, if at all; no public source gives how often they happen. Second, there is no public register of quay wall incidents, so the load events can only be checked against a few news items and notices. Third, the technical causes with the best data (G1, G2, P1, P3, WT, GW, M1, M2) are already inside the failure model and appear as one event M, so the table understates how much of the tree rests on data. Fourth, our tree splits the load branch more finely than the data allow: Q1 to Q3 and W2 are four assumptions for what is in effect one event, a load above the model value. The number of assumptions is therefore mainly a result of the available data, and only partly of the tree. A structured expert judgement, as the assignment allows, or the municipality's inspection and incident records, which we did not request, would replace most of them. A further difficulty is that pile condition is not a yes/no event, while a missed inspection is; we solved this by using the failure probability of the model as one event.

\textbf{Technical, human and organisational factors.} The technical part is best supported, by the failure model of Section~\ref{sec:prob}, although that model is calibrated on one Amsterdam case. The human part uses generic values, and the organisational part rests mostly on assumptions. Still, the organisational events matter: the repair chain lowers the probability from 0.20 to 0.11, and L2 to L4 are among the most important events. Because their inputs are weak, the size of this effect is uncertain.

\textbf{Conditions for conclusions and further data.} The conclusions hold for the ranking of causes and for comparing walls; the absolute probabilities only agree with the incidents in order of magnitude. The most useful extra data are pile condition from micro-drilling (M), canal-bed soundings and scour records (E3), the real programme order (L1), and an incident log of closures and emergency measures to check the result.

\section{Trade-off Analysis}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

\paragraph{Set-up.} The team used the fault tree of Section~\ref{sec:fault-tree}
to compare interventions for the median wall over the 15-year reference period
of NEN~8700 \parencite{nen8700}. Each intervention lowers one or more basic
events, and the new probabilities are propagated through the same tree, which
gives a new probability $P_f$ of the top event. The decision tree
(Figure~\ref{fig:dtree}) has one decision node with six alternatives, each
ending in a chance node with two outcomes: the programme does or does not
prevent a failure, which here means an emergency measure or a closure. The
objective is the lowest expected total cost
\begin{equation}
  E[C_i] = c_i + P_{f,i}\, C_f ,
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ [\euro] is the cost of intervention $i$ and $C_f$ [\euro] the cost
of a failure; costs are not discounted.

\paragraph{Interventions and costs.} Table~\ref{tab:interventions} lists the
alternatives, which cover the technical, human and organisational perspectives
of Section~\ref{sec:fault-tree}. How much a measure lowers an event is not
known from data, so one rule is assumed: a measure moves the events it acts on
to the lower bound of their range in Table~\ref{tab:ftprob}. Two exceptions
are also assumptions: the ring-fenced budget of A3 keeps L1 at 0 even when the
pace falls, and A4 replaces the foundation, so M becomes 0.01, close to the
NEN~8700 target for a new wall. The municipality provided no cost data, so
most costs are team estimates. Renewal costs about \euro1.3 million per wall:
the programme plan gives about \euro110 million for the wal- and kluismuren
\parencite{utrecht_programmaplan}, or \euro35k per metre over 3\,km, for a
36\,m wall. A2 adds \euro50k for micro-drilling and tilt sensors to the
\euro20k per wall that Amsterdam spends on investigating its quay walls
\parencite{amsterdam_brief}; A1 covers bed protection at about \euro2k per
metre, soundings, cameras and root barriers, and A3 one extra staff member for
15 years, shared over the 17 walls. The failure cost $C_f = $ \euro1.5
million combines a temporary safety structure of about \euro0.25 million per
wall \parencite{amsterdam_tender}, \euro0.65 million for the extra cost of an
urgent renewal and \euro0.6 million for closures, damage to cellars and
hindrance; as it is uncertain, it is varied from \euro0.5 to \euro10 million.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:results} and Figure~\ref{fig:dtree} give
the results. The current programme gives $P_f = 0.11$ and an expected cost of
\euro168k per wall. $P_f$ has two main parts: the model event combined with an
unrepaired weakness ($0.15 \times 0.35 = 0.05$) and scour E3 (0.05). Scour and
load control (A1) halves the load branch and lowers $P_f$ to 0.08, and
organisation (A3) lowers ``not repaired in time'' from 0.35 to 0.23 and $P_f$
to 0.10. Inspection (A2) hardly helps, because a missed inspection only
matters when the screening also misses the wall, which happens with
probability 0.19. Strengthening (A4) gives by far the lowest $P_f$ (0.04) at by
far the highest cost, and the package (A5) gives 0.05 for less than a quarter
of the cost of A4.

\paragraph{Risk-neutral and risk-averse choice.} A risk-neutral decision maker
looks only at $E[C]$, which with $C_f = $ \euro1.5 million favours A0: the extra
measures cost more than the damage they are expected to prevent. This choice
depends strongly on $C_f$: A1 is the cheapest above \euro3.4 million and A5
above \euro6.9 million, whereas A3 and A4 are never the cheapest below
\euro30 million. A public owner of heritage walls is more likely risk-averse,
which the team tested in two ways. With an exponential utility function,
$\mathrm{CE} = R \ln E[e^{C/R}]$ with risk tolerance $R = $ \euro0.5 million
(assumption), A1 and A0 have practically the same certainty equivalent
(\euro570k and \euro572k). In a pessimistic scenario with all events at their
upper bound, A1 is the cheapest (\euro421k), because scour (E3 = 0.15) then
dominates. The risk-neutral choice is therefore A0 and the risk-averse choice
A1, but the difference between them is small and depends mainly on the true
scour probability, which canal soundings can show.

\paragraph{Reflection.} The team expected the organisational measures to
matter, because L2 to L4 are among the most important events of
Section~\ref{sec:fault-tree}; they lower $P_f$ (A3: 0.11 to 0.10), but not
enough to pay for themselves. It did not expect the current programme to win
the risk-neutral comparison. This follows from the low $C_f$, since a failure
here is an emergency measure rather than a collapse, and not all objectives
can be put in euros: heritage value, accessibility, injuries and the trust of
residents are only partly in $C_f$, so its true value is probably higher,
which favours A1 and A5. The safety norm shows the same conflict: no
alternative brings $P_f$ below the NEN~8700 target of $\Phi(-1.8) = 0.036$.
A4 comes closest (0.037), but that value rests on the assumed M = 0.01, and
strengthening mainly brings forward a renewal that is due before 2050 anyway.

The inputs differ in quality. Event M comes from the failure model of
Section~\ref{sec:prob}, whose inputs are mostly literature values; the human
events I2 and I3 use generic HEART values, so the effect of A2 rests on general
numbers; and the organisational events are the weakest, as only L1 is derived
from data and 13 of the 19 events are assumptions. Scour E3 rests on a single
case: at its upper bound (0.15) A1 is clearly the best risk-neutral option, at
its lower bound (0.02) it is not worth its cost. The conclusions therefore hold
for comparing alternatives, not for absolute costs or probabilities, and they
assume independent walls, measures that work as soon as they are taken and the
lower-bound rule for their effect. Because the measures interact through the
AND and OR gates, they also have to be judged together, as in A5. Two conclusions are robust: inspection
alone (A2) is never the best, and strengthening every wall now (A4) is never
the best below $C_f = $ \euro30 million. The data that would help most are
real costs of renewals, emergency measures and incidents in Utrecht, to fix
$C_f$; canal soundings and a scour record, to estimate E3; data on permits and
cellar-owner consent, to check A3; and the wall type per wall, which would
allow strengthening only the weak type-1 walls and cheaper measures for the
rest.

\paragraph{Effectiveness of the interventions.} The measures lower the
probability of failure at a low cost. A1 lowers $P_f$ by about 30\,\% (0.11 to
0.08) and A3 by about 15\,\% (0.11 to 0.10), each for less than 10\,\% of the
cost of renewal, and A5 by more than half (0.11 to 0.05) for about 22\,\% of
that cost. No measure reaches the safety target, and inspection alone is not
effective. The most effective approach is therefore to add scour protection
where soundings show a risk (A1), to make the repair chain more reliable (A3),
and to keep strengthening for the walls that are shown to be weak.
